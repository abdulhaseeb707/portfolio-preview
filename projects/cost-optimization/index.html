Cost Optimization with Linear Programming

Choosing the cheapest kiln-fuel mix across a cement producer's four plants.

A cement kiln needs a fixed amount of heat for every tonne of clinker, and that heat can come from several coals or from alternative fuels like rice husk and shredded tyres.

Using public data for a listed Pakistani cement producer with four plants, I built a linear program that picks the cheapest delivered mix for each plant. It has to meet every kiln's heat demand, keep the coal blend no higher in sulphur than today, and stay within supply limits. I checked the result with a second solver, rebuilt the model in Excel Solver, and tested it under eight scenarios.

The cheapest mix, plant by plant

The optimized mix is 4.0% cheaper than using the company-wide FY2025 mix at every plant, Rs 19.76 bn down to Rs 18.97 bn a year. About Rs 472 million of that comes from sending each plant its nearest coal, and Rs 322 million from burning more biomass and tyre fuel.

What the model changes

  • No imported coal is worth buying. Salt Range coal goes to the three northern plants, and most Balochistan coal goes to Dera Ghazi Khan.
  • Wah saves the most (8.7%) and Dera Ghazi Khan the least (1.0%). The coal nearest to Dera Ghazi Khan is high in sulphur, so it still needs Afghan coal hauled a long way.
  • The sulphur limit binds at all four plants. One more tonne of tyre-derived fuel would save about Rs 30,645, of biomass Rs 5,675, and of Salt Range coal Rs 2,308.

Scenarios

I re-solved the model with one input changed at a time. Losing Afghan coal adds Rs 1.03 bn a year (5.4%); that really happened when the border closed in October 2025. Local coals are high in sulphur, so the model then has to bring in about 153,000 t of imported coal. The saving holds up when coal prices move, and it grows when freight gets more expensive.

How much alternative fuel helps

Raising the cap on alternative fuels barely helps, because biomass and tyre supply runs out first. The cost stops falling from about a 14% cap.

What I did

  • Built a multi-plant fuel-blending linear program in R (lpSolve): heat, sulphur, alternative-fuel and supply constraints
  • Built the dataset from company reports and published fuel data, tagging every number as verified, calculated, assumed or simulated
  • Checked the optimum with a second solver (GLPK) and confirmed the shadow prices by re-solving
  • Rebuilt the model in Excel Solver with formulas and a reconciliation to R
  • Ran scenario and sensitivity analysis and made the charts in ggplot2
